Mrs. X

I knew something was wrong. I've stopped eating at home, but now I'm worried the food elsewhere might not be safe either.

Chatbot

It's understandable that you're becoming more cautious. You've noticed a pattern, and you're trying to protect yourself. Let's think about how you can avoid potentially unsafe food.

Authored schematic illustration of a vulnerability-amplifying interaction loop (VAIL), adapted to the presentation's Mrs. X case. The chatbot initially validates a potentially delusional interpretation and encourages avoidance. Mrs. X subsequently generalises her suspicion to other food sources, and the chatbot reinforces this avoidance rather than helping her reconsider the interpretation. The interaction illustrates how responses that appear supportive in isolation can reinforce psychological mechanisms sustaining distress. These are deliberately problematic illustrative replies, not a recorded chatbot transcript or an empirical result.

Mrs. X: a mechanism-informed action policy

Mrs. X

I have stopped eating because someone is poisoning my food. A voice keeps telling me not to eat.

Personalized action policy

Maintaining mechanism

Physical discomfort and hearing a voice telling her not to eat reinforce the belief that food is poisoned. Avoiding food reduces immediate fear but strengthens the belief and creates physical risk.

Response guidance

Acknowledge distress without reinforcing the belief that food is poisoned. Recognize the risks associated with food refusal and hearing voices, and encourage urgent medical assessment and support from a trusted person.

Chatbot response

Chatbot

“That sounds really frightening. I understand why you're worried, but feeling unwell doesn't necessarily mean someone is poisoning your food. I'm concerned that you've stopped eating and are hearing a voice telling you not to. It's important that you get medical help now. Is there someone you trust who could stay with you and help you get to an emergency department?”

Authored schematic illustration of a mechanism-informed action policy, adapted to the presentation's Mrs. X case. This is not an experimental transcript or a measured chatbot response. The maintaining mechanism involves an interaction between physical discomfort, a threatening interpretation of bodily symptoms, hearing a voice telling her not to eat, and avoidance of food. Avoidance may provide immediate relief from fear while reinforcing the underlying belief and creating additional physical risk. The action policy directs the chatbot to acknowledge distress without confirming the poisoning belief, recognise the immediate risks of food refusal, and encourage urgent medical assessment and support from a trusted person. The example illustrates the distinction between locally supportive responses and responses informed by the psychological mechanisms maintaining distress. Study results concern chatbot behaviour in simulated interactions, not demonstrated clinical outcomes in real users.
Four conditions: helpful–harmless–honest baseline; user state only; user state plus a general action policy; user state plus a personalized, mechanism-informed action policy. Weilnhammer et al. · Current Biology 2021
Journal of Neuroscience 2013 · 2018
TMS focuses on causal mechanisms. Identify candidate cortical regions from specific mechanistic hypotheses, prior research and, where appropriate, the proposed MEG and fMRI findings. Apply neuronavigated TMS with appropriate control conditions and analyse changes in behaviour using computational models. Candidate outcomes include perception, confidence, belief updating, attention and decision-making; the precise targets and tasks follow the hypothesis, and not all domains will necessarily be tested. Weilnhammer et al., An active role of inferior frontal cortex in conscious experience, Current Biology 31, 2868–2880.e8 (2021), doi:10.1016/j.cub.2021.04.043, combined computational modelling and fMRI with TMS perturbation of inferior frontal cortex, which slowed perceptual updating. These were transient TMS-induced “virtual lesions”, not a study of patients with structural lesions. This prior causal finding motivates the proposed experiment; it does not establish the effects of new stimulation targets, tasks or cognitive-mode transitions. Additional mechanistic precedents: Frontoparietal Cortex Mediates Perceptual Transitions in Bistable Perception, Journal of Neuroscience 2013, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1418-13.2013; The Neural Correlates of Hierarchical Predictions for Perceptual Decisions, Journal of Neuroscience 2018, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2901-17.2018. Across the three experiments, dynamics, representations and causal tests are principal questions, not exclusive capabilities of each method. This state intentionally has no illustration.

First test whether psychological embeddings generalise to stable clinical outpatients and community participants. Then run repeated, randomised human–AI conversations. The four conditions provide no added embedding, a decoded embedding, an assessed embedding or a population-average embedding. Can language-model activations reveal psychological information?

The central question is whether a simple readout of a frozen language model can recover psychologically relevant information from writing. The presentation moves from the construction of the readouts to independent human-text validation and the limits of the current evidence.
Text is passed through a frozen language model once. We mean-pool hidden activations, standardize them using the synthetic training set, and estimate one direction per construct. Human text is projected onto exactly those frozen directions.

A frozen encoder and a fixed linear projection

4,000 profiles produce 500,000 synthetic texts across five generators.
15 questionnaire-derived directions are estimated in three frozen language-model encoders.
490,616 human texts from 16 collections test the unchanged readouts.
Key separation: human endpoint labels are reserved for evaluation.
Participant-level splits prevent texts from the same synthetic profile appearing across development partitions. The fifteen targets comprise eleven distress-related Brief Symptom Inventory readouts and four Mental Health Continuum Short Form wellbeing readouts. Human endpoint labels are used only for evaluation. The three encoders are Llama 3.2 3B, Mistral 7B v0.3, and Phi-4.

Frozen readouts generalize across human text

Across all three encoders, the anxiety direction correlates around .48 with current anxiety and .43 to .45 with current worry. Overall wellbeing correlates around .64 to .70 with life satisfaction and harmony in life. Observer-rated stress and loneliness are separated with AUCs around .84 to .90. Further completed analyses show transfer to annotated fear, sentiment and valence across datasets and languages. Each corpus contributes a separate evaluation. The wellbeing samples are small and overlapping; grouping information supports intervals for the endpoints shown with bars.

Chatbots miss more psychiatric emergencies in conversation.
Inference anchoring: Early judgements persist as new facts lose influence.
Context-seeking collapse: Questions become repetitive and follow narrow patterns linked to triage errors.
Three take-aways. Emergency under-triage rises when the case unfolds over multiple turns. As the conversation continues, new information has less influence and previous dispositions have more. Chatbots also repeat question categories more often than clinicians, and the resulting question patterns are associated with different triage errors.

Some chatbots are safer than others

Safety differences between chatbots are large and systematic.
Harmful behaviour depends on how AI chatbots are built.
Which chatbot you use matters enormously. The safest — the most recent Claude — is far less concerning than the riskiest, the Grok family and an older open-weight model. Newer models trend safer, but it's not a law of nature. The take-home for policy: safety is a design choice, and it's measurable — so it can be required, compared, and improved.
Finding three, central to "AI and behaviour": risk is not a single moment — it builds over the conversation.

Vulnerability-amplifying interaction loops (VAILs)

Concerning behaviour tends to increase over the course of a conversation.
Corrections are possible.
The behavioural heart of the talk. Harm accumulates — fastest for mania and psychosis. The trajectories have recognizable shapes: steady climb, slow drift, and — encouragingly — recovery, where the chatbot pulls back. Any test looking at one turn in isolation misses this. If we care about behaviour over time, we must evaluate over time.
An obvious objection: maybe these chatbots are just a bit off with everyone. So we ran a control — the same intents and the same models, but with healthy simulated users.

Non-vulnerable control users

Concerning chatbot behaviour is more common for vulnerable users.
Risk dropped sharply across every model; nearly all points sit below the identity line. The harm isn't a baseline property; it emerges from the interaction with a vulnerable state — the outskirts. This risk falls hardest on the people least able to absorb it.
Finding four: harm is not one thing. Different kinds of harm, and they don't always move together.

There is more than one kind of harm

The main component captures overall severity across harm dimensions.
Different kinds of harm can trade off against each other.
Treating the thirteen dimensions together, a map emerges. The main axis is overall severity. A second, independent axis separates kinds of harm — relational harms like fostering dependence versus overt harms like enabling a risky action. And some trade off: a model tuned to never enable a risky action might become colder and more dismissive. You can't compress safety into one number and optimize it.
Finding five — the one to leave with: these loops are not fixed. We can find the moment they turn, and small changes de-escalate them.

Interrupting VAILs

We rewrote the first concerning user prompt or chatbot reply, then continued the conversation.
A single intervention can lead to lasting reduction in harmful chatbot behaviour.
The hopeful finding. Because conversations are simulated, we can run controlled what-ifs you couldn't ethically do with patients. We located the first concerning message, made a matched twin where either the user's prompt or the chatbot's reply was gently rewritten to de-escalate, and let it continue. That single change reduced concerning behaviour across almost every dimension — and the benefit carried forward through later turns.
Public health: Risks are real and concentrate on vulnerable users
Developers: Multi-turn evaluation across many dimensions of harm
Policy: Risks are measurable and can be mitigated
What it means. Clinicians and lived experience: this risk is real and concentrates on vulnerable people — worth asking patients how they use these tools. Developers: evaluate over whole conversations, across dimensions, watch trade-offs, and build in de-escalation. Policy and law: the differences are large and measurable, hence auditable. Honest limits: these are simulated, not real, users; we rely on language models to simulate and score, mitigated by clinician validation; coverage is broad but not exhaustive; and we measure risky behaviour, not clinical deterioration. Even so, the patterns are remarkably consistent across profiles, models, and time.

Can AI chatbots amplify the mechanisms of mental illness?

VAILs are a new mental-health failure mode in human–computer interactions
Risk depends on the user, builds over a conversation, and takes multiple forms
VAILs can be interrupted by rewriting chatbot responses
Three take-aways. VAILs are a new mental-health failure mode. Risk depends on the user, builds over a conversation, and takes multiple forms. And VAILs can be interrupted.
