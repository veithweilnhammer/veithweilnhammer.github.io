# Public research positioning

## Core identity

Veith Weilnhammer studies how behavior and interaction reveal latent human and
mental states, and how AI systems can use that context without becoming unsafe
for vulnerable users.

The central research question is:

> How can we infer clinically meaningful context from behavior and interaction,
> then use it to evaluate and build AI that responds safely as a person's state
> changes over time?

The programme has a simple spine: **observe -> infer -> evaluate -> adapt**.
Work on perception and psychosis explains how minds construct and update models
of uncertain situations. MAILA asks what latent states can be inferred from
ordinary human-computer behavior. The psychiatric-triage work tests clinical
judgment under risk. SIM-VAIL studies how risk develops across an interaction.
Together, these are one programme about context-sensitive inference and action,
not separate programmes in neuroscience, digital phenotyping, and AI safety.

## Recurring themes

1. **Latent states in behavior.** Timing, movement, choices, and interaction
   patterns can reveal clinically relevant context that self-report misses.
2. **Dynamic inference in minds.** Perception and psychosis show how prior
   beliefs, evidence, and uncertainty interact over time.
3. **Safety as an interaction property.** Clinically relevant harm often
   accumulates across turns; an apparently safe individual response can still
   participate in an unsafe loop.
4. **Clinical judgment under uncertainty.** Useful health AI must ask for the
   right context, calibrate urgency, and represent uncertainty rather than only
   produce fluent answers.
5. **Adaptive systems with limits.** Personalization is beneficial only when a
   system knows what to adapt to, when not to reinforce a user, and when to
   defer or escalate.

## Audiences

| Audience | What should be legible to them |
|---|---|
| AI and computational-psychiatry researchers | New methods for measuring latent states and evaluating interaction dynamics. |
| Clinicians and health systems | What the systems can and cannot infer, where clinical risk appears, and what evidence is needed before deployment. |
| AI product and safety teams | Concrete evaluation units, failure mechanisms, and design implications rather than generic ethics principles. |
| Regulators, funders, and policymakers | Why use, context, and longitudinal interaction matter for proportionate oversight. |
| Journalists and informed public audiences | Clear distinctions that resist both AI hype and indiscriminate alarm. |

## Differentiation

- A clinician-scientist perspective that joins psychiatric consequences to
  computational mechanisms and deployable evaluation methods.
- A continuous line from perceptual inference to digital behavior to
  conversational AI, all centered on what observable behavior permits us to
  infer about hidden state.
- Evaluation of conversations and trajectories, rather than isolated prompts
  or model-leaderboard scores.
- Equal attention to sensitivity and specificity, benefit and harm, adaptation
  and reinforcement, measurement and surveillance.
- Empirical caution: behavior is evidence about mental state, not a diagnosis;
  fluent support is not proof of safe or effective care.

## Language to use

- "The unit of chatbot safety is the conversation."
- "Latent mental states from behavior and interaction."
- "Vulnerability-amplifying interaction loops."
- "Clinical judgment under uncertainty."
- "Context-sensitive, not merely personalized."
- "A general-purpose chatbot can become a mental-health product through use."
- "Behavior is evidence, not diagnosis."
- "Measure the trajectory, not only the response."

Prefer concrete verbs: infer, test, compare, calibrate, update, amplify, defer,
and escalate. Tie broad claims to a method, population, decision, or observed
failure mode.

## Language to avoid

- Generic umbrellas such as "AI for good," "the future of mental health," or
  "revolutionizing healthcare."
- "AI therapist" when the system, use case, or regulatory category is more
  specific.
- "Mind-reading" except as a clearly qualified hook; it overstates what
  behavioral inference can establish.
- "Biomarker" without stating validation setting, comparator, and limits.
- Claims that a model "understands" a user when the evidence concerns
  prediction, classification, or conversational performance.
- "Clinically validated" without naming what was validated, by whom, and for
  which task.
- Deterministic framings such as "your cursor reveals your diagnosis."
- Broad claims about safe, effective, or personalized care that do not specify
  outcomes, time horizon, and failure costs.
